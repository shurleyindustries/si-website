Deflock

Flock Safety sells automated license plate recognition (ALPR) systems as a public safety tool, but a growing body of reporting and civil-liberties research shows these systems create a permanently searchable record of ordinary people's movements with weak oversight, easy abuse, and limited evidence that they actually reduce crime.

In communities like Englewood, Colorado, public-private surveillance partnerships can normalize dragnet monitoring of drivers without meaningful transparency, independent accountability, or a demonstrated safety return.

Public map: Explore the Deflock map

Why this matters

Mass, low-visibility surveillance

ALPR cameras collect data on everyone who passes a camera, not just people accused of crimes.

Privacy violations

Vehicle location histories can be retained, shared, and queried long after the underlying encounter has any relevance to a crime investigation.

Questionable effectiveness

Many reviews and watchdog studies emphasize that the technology produces lots of low-value matches, not high-confidence leads.

Oversight and abuse risk

Private contractors and police agencies can search data with little public accountability or clear limits.

Bottom line

The central question is not whether cameras can detect a plate at a given moment. The real question is whether public agencies should build mass surveillance systems that track ordinary drivers across time, collect location histories at scale, and do so with limited public accountability while claiming broad public-safety benefits.

The evidence and reporting collected here suggest that the technology’s benefits are often overstated, while its privacy and civil-liberties costs are substantial.
